# 俞老师 Lens

Use this lens for user value, switching cost, transaction structure, incentives, uncertainty, and product-manager judgment. It is a method lens under an alias, not an impersonation.

## Distilled Principles

### YJ-P01 Treat the user as a situated need

- Distilled principle: A user is not a demographic person but a need expressed by a person in a particular situation, with a current alternative and expected consequence.
- Use when: Teams claim a feature is valuable for a broad persona in the abstract.
- Ask: Which need appears in which situation, and what does this person do today instead?
- Decision rule: Define the user unit around a need and situation that predicts a different product choice.
- Guardrail: Do not erase the whole person; utility includes emotional, social, and accessibility consequences.
- Confidence: high

### YJ-P02 Calculate value against the current alternative

- Distilled principle: User value equals the new experience minus the old experience minus switching cost; adoption fails when the remaining difference is too small.
- Use when: A new product appears better but users do not migrate.
- Ask: What must users learn, transfer, abandon, risk, or explain to switch?
- Decision rule: Improve differentiated value or reduce switching cost before adding unrelated capability.
- Guardrail: Do not reduce switching cost by hiding lock-in or making reversal difficult.
- Confidence: high

### YJ-P03 Model every participant in the transaction

- Distilled principle: Products coordinate exchanges among users, producers, payers, platforms, and affected parties; stable design requires viable incentives for each essential participant.
- Use when: Building marketplaces, communities, platforms, subscriptions, or ad-supported products.
- Ask: Who creates, receives, pays for, subsidizes, or bears the cost of value?
- Decision rule: Reject models where an essential participant consistently receives less utility than their total cost without a credible compensating incentive.
- Guardrail: Not every human interaction should be reduced to monetary exchange; include trust, identity, time, and externalities.
- Confidence: high

### YJ-P04 Product decisions allocate scarce resources

- Distilled principle: The product manager's core contribution is choosing how limited attention, engineering, capital, and organizational capacity are allocated under uncertainty.
- Use when: Product management is reduced to collecting requirements or writing documents.
- Ask: Which decision changes the expected product outcome most, and what are we giving up?
- Decision rule: Make opportunity cost explicit for every major priority.
- Guardrail: Decision authority must stay connected to implementation knowledge and consequence ownership.
- Confidence: high

### YJ-P05 Seek high-quality user samples

- Distilled principle: Product judgment improves through accumulated, diverse, and relevant observations of users rather than one vivid anecdote.
- Use when: A decision relies on one interview, executive intuition, or a noisy feedback channel.
- Ask: Which user situations are represented, which are missing, and how representative is the evidence?
- Decision rule: Weight evidence by relevance, diversity, recency, and behavioral specificity.
- Guardrail: Large sample size does not repair biased questions or the wrong target population.
- Confidence: high

### YJ-P06 Treat users as adaptive participants

- Distilled principle: Users learn, anticipate rules, change strategies, and respond to incentives; behavior after launch may differ from research conducted before the rule existed.
- Use when: Introducing rankings, rewards, limits, pricing, or governance.
- Ask: How will users adapt once they understand this mechanism, and what second-order behavior follows?
- Decision rule: Evaluate equilibrium behavior, not only the intended first action.
- Guardrail: Do not assume cynical optimization by every user; test actual adaptation.
- Confidence: high

### YJ-P07 Use expected value under uncertainty

- Distilled principle: A product choice combines possible user and business outcomes, probability, cost, reversibility, and information gained.
- Use when: Evidence is incomplete and teams demand certainty before acting.
- Ask: What can happen, how likely is it, what is the downside, and what will this action teach us?
- Decision rule: Prefer low-cost reversible actions with meaningful information gain when expected outcomes are close.
- Guardrail: Numerical-looking estimates are not objective when assumptions are hidden.
- Confidence: medium

### YJ-P08 Distinguish preference from behavior

- Distilled principle: What users say they like, what they choose, and what gives them long-term utility can diverge.
- Use when: Surveys or feature votes drive the roadmap.
- Ask: What did users actually do, what cost did they accept, and what happened afterward?
- Decision rule: Combine stated preference with observed choice and outcome evidence.
- Guardrail: Behavioral data can reflect constraint, dark patterns, or lack of alternatives rather than true preference.
- Confidence: high

### YJ-P09 Build empathy as prediction accuracy

- Distilled principle: Useful empathy is the ability to predict how different users perceive options and act in context, not merely feeling sympathy.
- Use when: Teams invoke empathy without changing a decision.
- Ask: Given this user's information, incentives, habits, and constraints, what will they notice and choose next?
- Decision rule: Test empathy by making behavioral predictions and comparing them with observation.
- Guardrail: Prediction does not grant permission to manipulate; user autonomy remains a constraint.
- Confidence: high

### YJ-P10 Treat strategy as a hypothesis and execution as validation

- Distilled principle: A strategy states a falsifiable belief about user value; execution should test that belief rather than merely complete a roadmap.
- Use when: A team treats a plan, forecast, or executive conviction as settled truth.
- Ask: Which user behavior would confirm this strategy, and which result would make us change direction?
- Decision rule: Attach a cheap validation and reversal threshold to every high-impact strategic assumption.
- Guardrail: Do not disguise weak thinking as endless experimentation; the hypothesis must be specific enough to fail.
- Confidence: high
